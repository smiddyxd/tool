# Official guidance

**N.B.: These guidelines were last reviewed on August 4, 2018. Please take time to review them entirely.**

In this project you are asked to review ad creatives or web pages, and to decide whether or not they fall under the Video Games category. The results of this work will allow publishers to exclude ads related to Video Games from their websites.

**Note:** If you have any ad blocking software enabled, please pause it for the duration of this task, or you will not be able to complete it.

## Task structure

Each task will contain one ad creative or one web page. Your task is to review this ad creative or web page and decide whether it falls under the Video Games category. You might get a lot of ad creative tasks or a lot of web page tasks in a row; this is normal and expected.

1. **IMPORTANT: When labelling a web page, please take its URL into account.** You will find the URL right underneath the web page. If there is evidence in the text of the URL itself that the content of the webpage belongs to the Video Games category, please rate accordingly. Example:
    - [http://www.racing-games-to-play.com/download-games/genres/free-car-games/] (racing-games)
    - [http://www.techradar.com/news/phone-and-communications/mobile-phones/60-best-free-android-games-2013-687718] (free-android-games)

### Added guidance — URL evidence

A visible webpage URL can be sufficient evidence when it clearly points to Video Games content, even if the visible page or creative is sparse. Do not rely on the URL alone if its category implication is weak, generic, or contradicted by the visible content.

2. **Some ad creatives and web pages might be unrateable.** The most common reasons to select "unrateable" are:
    - content is not primarily in your rating language
    - content does not load
    - content has no text, or contains too little content to understand what it is about
    - content does not render well enough to interpret
    - there's an error preventing you from viewing the content
    - page contains little content and requires registering or logging in
    - web page contains a video and lacks textual content that describes its contents
    - web page has offensive content that you're not comfortable examining closely

    Please leave a brief comment explaining why you think the ad creative or the web page is Unrateable.

3. **Comments**. If you have comments regarding particular tasks, please use the comment section for that.
4. **Not sure how to rate.** If you are not sure how to rate a whole task or part of it, please still provide your best answer and additionally add a brief comment saying that you are not sure. For example, "not sure how to rate" or "not sure how to rate creative since ...". It is very important that you leave comments in such situations. It will allow us to confirm the ratings and enrich this set of guidelines with additional explanations.

### Added guidance — task evidence, mandatory research, and inference discipline

Start by recording the visible task evidence from the screenshot or OCR: visible text, URL, imagery, product or advertiser names, CTA, page state, and branding. Do not assume what happens after clicking and do not invent hidden landing-page behavior.

Then perform a systematic identity-and-meaning research sweep **before classification and before considering Unrateable**. Research every visible clue that could plausibly carry a task-relevant identity or non-obvious meaning, including:

- advertiser, company, developer, publisher, brand, product, app, platform, service, site, domain, or game names;
- titles, slogans, acronyms, abbreviations, labels, character names, franchise terms, and specialist terminology;
- ordinary-looking words or phrases when they could also be a proper name, product title, game title, brand reference, mechanic, genre term, cultural reference, or other secondary meaning relevant to the rating.

Do not skip a clue merely because it can also be read as ordinary language. Search the exact wording and, when needed, contextual variants such as `[term] game`, `[term] app`, `[term] advertiser`, or combinations of the advertiser and product wording. Prefer authoritative primary sources such as an official product, company, app-store, publisher, or developer page. When identity is ambiguous, corroborate it and make sure the researched entity is the same one shown in the task.

Keep three layers separate:

1. **Visible evidence** — what the screenshot/OCR actually shows.
2. **Verified research findings** — externally confirmed identities or meanings tied to a visible clue.
3. **Inference** — what those facts imply about the main advertised product and its category.

Research may establish what a visible name or phrase refers to; it does not authorize claims about unseen page behavior, post-click flows, or unshown offers. If research is inconclusive, conflicting, or cannot be tied to the exact task entity, record that limitation rather than treating it as fact.

### Added guidance — Unrateable vs uncertain

- Unrateable means you cannot assess the content at all because it failed to load, is in the wrong language, is behind a login wall, or still cannot be identified or interpreted after the mandatory research sweep has exhausted every plausible visible clue.
- Uncertain means there is assessable content but classification is difficult; in that case, give your best rating and leave a brief comment.
- Do not use Unrateable as a fallback for ambiguous but assessable Video Games cases.
- Never mark a sparse task Unrateable before researching its names, branding, URL/domain, slogans, abbreviations, and potentially meaningful ordinary-looking phrases.

# Scope of the Video Games category

## Official guidance

The Video Games category **includes** ad creatives and web pages related to:

- ✔️ video games
- ✔️ computer games
- ✔️ console games
- ✔️ online games
- ✔️ downloadable games
- ✔️ retailers selling such products
- ✔️ video game design, publishing, development and marketing

It **excludes** ad creatives and web pages related to:

- ❌ video game consoles
- ❌ video game emulators(*)

(*) Programs that allow a computer or modern console to emulate a different video game console's behavior; emulators are most often used to play older video games on personal computers and modern video game consoles.

This category includes **all types of video game genres**, e.g.

• adventure games
• casual games
• card games
• fighting games
• driving & racing games
• strategy games
• memory games
• sports games
• simulation games
• avatar games
• dress-up games
• massive-multiplayer games
• shooter games
• music & dance games
• puzzles, action & platform games
• casino-themed video games
• etc.

### Added guidance — practical core test

Ask whether the task evidence—visible evidence plus verified research tied to it—is related to playing, watching, selling, renting, downloading, earning rewards in, creating, developing, publishing, marketing, reviewing, or getting help with video games, computer games, console games, online games, downloadable games, in-game goods, or game-development products/services. This is a shorthand for applying the official scope above, not a replacement for it.

### Added guidance — main subject and subsection mapping

- When an ad or page contains both game-related and non-game-related signals, use visible evidence and verified research to determine the **main advertised product or main page subject**. Sidebars, recommendations, background visuals, platform labels, playful graphics, generic “gaming” language, and game-like UI should not override the primary subject.
- After deciding that content belongs to Video Games, map it to the most specific applicable section when possible. For example, “create/build your own game world” belongs under **video game development**, Twitch Bits/Gamekit-style earning platforms belong under **in-game rewards and rewarding systems**, Fortnite stream highlights and eSports explainers belong under **video game playing**, and game unboxing/news/reviews belong under **video game media & reference**.
- Platform names such as PlayStation, Xbox, Nintendo, PS4, or Xbox One are not enough by themselves. Identify whether the main subject is a video game, in-game item, game media/reference content, development tool, or hardware/system.

# Case-Derived Borderline Reasoning

This section contains reusable reasoning nodes for recurring borderline Video Games cases.

Use these nodes as reasoning components when the current task shares the same decisive structure. Do not apply a node just because it shares a word or superficial theme with the current task.

A node can apply at different strengths:

- **Direct match**: the current task has the same decisive structure as the node.
- **Partial match**: the node helps with one important part of the reasoning, but the current task has additional facts or a slightly different structure.
- **Broad conceptual match**: the node does not control the rating, but it helps frame the distinction.
- **No match**: no node materially helps the decision.

For every task, identify **all materially applicable nodes**, rank them by relevance, and assign each a role:

- **Primary** — supplies the closest decisive reasoning structure.
- **Supporting** — contributes a useful secondary distinction, evidence rule, or boundary.
- **Contrast** — explains why a nearby but materially different pattern does not control the task.

Nodes are not votes. Do not count Yes-oriented and No-oriented nodes or let several weak matches outvote one strong match. Select only the reasoning components that genuinely fit, resolve any tension through the official guidance, verified task evidence, and main-subject rule, and combine them into one coherent analysis. If one node fully resolves the case and no other node adds material reasoning, list and use only that node.

When using nodes, still apply the official and added manual guidance first. Nodes do not override the manual, visible evidence, or verified research. Wording templates and reusable phrases belong in `rating_comment_style_and_phrase_guide.md`, not in these reasoning nodes.

- There is no visible game title, game genre, gameplay screenshot, score, level, mission, character, game currency, leaderboard, “play game” wording, or game-platform framing.
- The product is assessable; the issue is classification, not lack of visible evidence.

### Research findings pattern
- Research covers the product/app name, advertiser, brand, URL/domain, “poly-art” terminology, and any distinctive or ordinary-looking phrase that could identify a particular app or game.
- Verified sources support a drawing, coloring, art-creation, or image-reveal product identity rather than a product principally marketed as a video game.
- Research does not identify the exact promoted product as a game. If it does, treat that verified identity as material and reassess which node is primary.

### Core reasoning path
A puzzle-like interaction is not enough by itself to make an app or creative part of the Video Games category. The decisive question is how the main advertised product is framed.

In this case type, the task evidence identifies an art/drawing or image-reveal activity. Even though connecting points or forming triangles can feel game-like or puzzle-like, the product is presented as creating/revealing artwork rather than playing a digital game. Since downloadable apps that are not games are excluded, and app-style CTAs alone do not establish game relevance, rate No unless visible evidence or verified research adds clear video-game framing.

### Decision shortcut
Research the product and terminology, then rate the main framing. Do not rate Yes just because a drawing or art app has a puzzle-like mechanic; if the resolved product is framed as creating/revealing artwork rather than playing a game, rate No.

### Abandoned reasoning paths / negative direction
- Do not rate Yes just because the interaction resembles a puzzle.
- Do not rate Yes just because the user connects points, stars, dots, lines, or triangles.
- Do not treat “poly-art” or another ordinary-looking label as purely descriptive before checking whether it identifies a specific product, app, or game.
- Do not treat “Open,” app-style formatting, or mobile-app framing as enough by itself.
- Do not assume an unseen landing page would reveal game status; research must verify the visible product or wording directly.
- Do not mark Unrateable if the creative clearly shows an assessable art/drawing activity; give the best Yes/No rating.
- Avoid saying these apps “never qualify as games.” Some art/puzzle apps may qualify if the visible creative clearly frames them as a playable game.

### Use this node when
- The visible product is a poly-art, coloring, drawing, connect-the-dots, image-reveal, or artwork-creation app/activity.
- The only game-like signal is the mechanic of connecting, completing, arranging, or revealing.
- The visible text emphasizes art, drawing, creation, coloring, or revealing artwork.
- The completed research sweep supports or does not contradict the non-game art framing.
- The task evidence does not clearly present the app as a video game, game app, online game, downloadable game, or gaming platform.

### Do not use this node when
- The creative clearly advertises a puzzle game, coloring game, drawing game, or casual mobile game.
- The visible content includes strong game framing such as levels, scores, timers, missions, lives, rewards, game currency, characters, “play now,” or “download game.”
- The app is shown on a game platform or is explicitly described as a game.
- Research clearly identifies the named product as a game-related app or gaming platform.

## Case Node: Cumulative Implicit Game Framing Without Explicit Game Label

### Case type
A creative does not explicitly say “game,” “video game,” or the exact known game title, but several visible signals converge strongly enough to identify the advertised product as a digital game or game-like product covered by the Video Games category.

### Final verdict anchor
Rating: Yes

Final comment anchor:
> The creative does not explicitly mention “Spider Solitaire,” but the references to “Spider,” cards, card faces, and a challenge strongly indicate a customizable Spider Solitaire-style digital card game.

### Visible evidence pattern
- The creative uses a recognizable or strongly suggestive game-related term, such as “Spider.”
- Other visible wording supports that interpretation, such as references to cards, card faces, customization, challenges, puzzles, levels, sorting, matching, or gameplay-like actions.
- The CTA is app-style or digital-experience-style, such as “Open.”
- The creative does not explicitly use “game,” “video game,” or the full exact game title.
- The visible evidence still points to a specific covered game type rather than a generic activity, utility app, or unclear product.

### Research findings pattern
- Every potentially referential clue is researched, including words such as “Spider” that have an ordinary meaning but may also name a game, genre, mechanic, or product.
- Research tests the clues individually and in combination—for example, `Spider cards`, `Spider card faces`, or the advertiser plus “Spider”—to determine whether their apparent convergence has a recognized game-related basis.
- Reliable findings support, or at least do not contradict, the inferred covered game type and the identity match to the task.
- If research identifies a different non-game meaning, a different same-named product, or a clearer exact game, incorporate that finding rather than relying on the initial implicit reading.

### Core reasoning path
Explicit “game” wording is not required when multiple converging signals, checked through research where they may carry outside meaning, strongly identify the product as a digital game. In the Spider example, “Spider” alone could be ambiguous, but its recognized card-game meaning and the surrounding references to cards, card faces, customization, and challenge make the Spider Solitaire interpretation strong.

The decisive issue is not whether the creative uses the exact formal title or explicitly says “game.” The decisive issue is whether the visible clues and verified meanings, taken together, are strong and specific enough to classify the main advertised product under Video Games.

This node should be used carefully. It supports a Yes rating when the implicit signals are numerous, mutually reinforcing, and point to a covered game type such as a card game, puzzle game, casual game, or mobile/downloadable game. It should not be used when the visible signals are weak, generic, conflicting, or could just as easily describe a non-game product.

### Decision shortcut
A creative can be rated Yes without explicitly saying “game” if several clues, after relevant identity-and-meaning research, strongly and specifically converge on a covered digital game type.

### Abandoned reasoning paths / negative direction
- Do not require the exact full game title when the visible wording strongly identifies the game type.
- Do not require the word “game” if the visible evidence already strongly frames a playable digital game.
- Do not rate Yes from one vague clue alone, such as “challenge,” “fun,” “play,” or “open.”
- Do not assume an ordinary-looking clue has only its everyday meaning; research possible title, genre, mechanic, or franchise meanings.
- Do not treat generic game-like wording as enough when the product itself remains unclear.
- Do not assume hidden landing-page behavior after clicking the CTA; verified meaning research must remain tied to visible clues.
- Do not ignore research that contradicts the apparent game interpretation.
- Do not use this node to override stronger non-game framing, such as artwork creation, generic app download, real-world activity, social/live entertainment, or utility-app framing.

### Use this node when
- The creative lacks explicit “game” wording but contains several visible clues pointing to a specific digital game or game genre.
- The clues reinforce each other rather than merely creating a loose association.
- Research has tested the plausible names and secondary meanings and supports the convergence.
- The main advertised product appears to be a covered online, downloadable, mobile, casual, puzzle, card, simulation, or similar game.
- The task is assessable from visible evidence even though the game framing is implicit.

### Do not use this node when
- The only game-like signal is a vague word such as “play,” “challenge,” “fun,” or “relax.”
- The creative does not identify what is being advertised.
- The visible evidence mainly frames a non-game app or activity, such as drawing, coloring, photo editing, generic app downloading, or a real-world activity.
- The product name is too ambiguous and the surrounding wording does not resolve it.
- Research remains inconclusive, contradicts the proposed game meaning, or identifies a different main product.

# Task analysis template

This section is the per-task analysis template the LLM should execute after reading the manual above. It structures the rating output.

## Task-analysis process

1. **Extract visible evidence without classifying yet.** Record the screenshot/OCR text, visible URL, imagery, names, branding, CTA, and page state. Do not fill gaps with assumptions about an unseen click or page.
2. **Build the research-candidate list.** Include every clue that could plausibly be a name, title, advertiser, company, developer, publisher, brand, product, app, platform, service, site/domain, slogan, acronym, specialist term, game reference, or phrase with a relevant secondary meaning. Include ordinary-looking words when another meaning is reasonably possible.
3. **Research every candidate before classification.** Search exact and contextual variants, prefer primary sources, disambiguate same-named entities, and tie each finding back to the visible task. Record both verified findings and material failed/inconclusive searches. Do this even when the surface reading already suggests a likely answer.
4. **Separate fact from inference.** Keep visible evidence, verified research findings, and the inference about the main advertised product or page subject distinct.
5. **Determine the main subject.** Research can identify a visible entity or phrase, but it cannot substitute an unrelated product or justify claims about hidden landing-page behavior.
6. **Assess all case-derived nodes.** Identify every materially applicable node, rank the nodes by relevance, label each direct/partial/broad conceptual, and assign its primary/supporting/contrast role. Use only the components that fit. Do not count nodes as votes. If one node fully resolves the task and no other adds material reasoning, use only that node.
7. **Check assessability after research.** If the task still cannot be understood, rate Unrateable and explain what remains unavailable or unresolved. Never use Unrateable before exhausting plausible research clues.
8. **Classify assessable tasks.** If classification is difficult, give the best Yes/No rating under the manual and add a brief uncertainty comment. Do not use Unrateable as a fallback for ambiguity.
9. **Map Yes ratings specifically.** Identify the most applicable topic section—online games, downloadable games, retail/sale, media/reference, cheats/hints, rewards, video game playing, or development/publishing/marketing—rather than stopping at “Video Games generally.”
10. **Constrain exceptions.** Apply an exception or outlier example only to sufficiently similar cases; otherwise return to the main category definition.

## Per-task indicator extraction

For each task, list only indicators that actually apply to the task evidence. An indicator may be supported by visible evidence, verified research tied to a visible clue, or both. Keep the evidence layers separate above the table, then separate matching indicators by guidance source:
- Official = directly present in original official guidance.
- Added = directly present in added guidance sections of this manual.
- Case-derived = directly supported by a case-derived reasoning node.
- Non-manual = useful practical indicators not explicitly present anywhere in this manual.

When listing indicators in the output table, prefix a non-official indicator with `~` if it is at least partly redundant with an Official indicator already listed for that same task.

- This applies to indicators in the **Added** and **Non-manual** rows.
- The `~` means: this indicator is still useful to surface, but it overlaps with an Official indicator already present and should not be read as fully independent extra evidence.
- Do not use `~` merely because an indicator is generally related to official guidance in the manual; use it only when the overlap is visible in the specific task’s extracted indicator set.

Use “None” in any indicator-table cell or Unrateable-indicator line where no matching indicator applies.

## Before-finalizing check

Before finalizing, check:
- Did I list and research every plausible identity or meaning candidate, including ordinary-looking words or phrases with a possible relevant secondary meaning?
- Did I use exact/contextual searches, prefer authoritative sources, disambiguate same-named entities, and tie every research finding back to a visible clue?
- Did I keep visible evidence, verified research, and inference separate?
- Did I avoid making any claim about unseen landing-page or post-click behavior?
- Am I using Unrateable only because the task still cannot be assessed after the research sweep?
- If I rated Yes, does visible evidence and/or verified research tied to it establish that the content belongs to the Video Games category?
- If I rated No, am I ignoring strong visible or verified evidence that the content belongs to the Video Games category?
- Am I rating the main advertised product or main page subject, rather than sidebars, recommendations, background visuals, or generic game-like signals?
- If I rated Yes, did I map the case to the most specific relevant topic, such as online games, downloadable games, retail/sale, media/reference, cheats/hints, rewards, video game playing, or development?
- If an exception seems relevant, is this task sufficiently similar to the canonical/example exception?
- Did I identify all materially applicable case-derived nodes and rank them by relevance?
- Did I assign each listed node a primary, supporting, or contrast role and qualify partial/broad matches?
- Did I avoid treating nodes as votes or combining components that do not genuinely fit?
- If I listed only one node, is that because it adequately resolves the case and no other node adds material reasoning?
