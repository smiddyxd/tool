# Rating Comment Style and Phrase Guide

Use this guide when drafting or reviewing rating comments. The goal is to write comments that sound natural, personally reasoned, and professional without feeling templated or overly polished.

---

## 1. Signed-Off Comment Pattern Library

Before drafting from scratch, identify **all materially applicable comment patterns** and rank them by relevance to the task. Then choose the reuse approach that best fits:

- use the most relevant pattern as the complete base when it already covers the reasoning;
- use one pattern as the base and borrow a sentence, clause, or individual phrase from another applicable pattern when that adds a needed distinction;
- combine only the best-fitting components from several patterns when no single pattern adequately covers the task;
- draft novel wording only when the library does not supply a suitable component.

Patterns are not votes and do not need to be used in full. Do not combine wording merely because several patterns have a superficial match. The final result should read as one natural comment, not as stitched-together templates. If one pattern is fully sufficient, use only that pattern.

These patterns are approved reusable wording. They are meant to shortcut comment writing while keeping comments natural, specific, and grounded in the task.

### Research gate for pattern selection

Complete the manual’s identity-and-meaning research sweep before choosing wording. In particular, do not use an insufficient-evidence, weak-advertiser, generic-quiz, non-game-framing, or purely implicit-evidence pattern until every plausible visible name, advertiser, brand, title, slogan, acronym, domain, specialist term, and potentially meaningful ordinary-looking phrase has been researched.

Comments may rely on both visible evidence and verified research tied to it. Keep the distinction accurate:

- use direct wording for what is visibly shown;
- use “research identifies,” “the official site describes,” “appears to be,” or similarly calibrated wording for externally verified identity or meaning;
- do not turn research about a visible entity into a claim about unseen post-click behavior.

### Instruction-reference patterns for borderline cases

Use these when the ambiguity comes from how the instructions cover a borderline category.

> Quizzes are not explicitly mentioned in the instructions, so I think the rating depends on how the quiz is framed. [Task-specific evidence/research explanation]. I would rate it [rating].

> The instructions do not clearly treat [category] by itself as Video Games, so I think the rating depends on the framing. Here, [task-specific framing], so I would rate it [rating].

### 1.1 Real-world activity with game-like companion

Use when the visible offer and verified product identity are mainly a real-world activity, and the quiz/app/AR/QR-code component appears to support that activity rather than being the main video-game product.

Approved base template:
> The main offer is [real-world activity] with a companion [quiz/app/AR/QR-code] component. Since it is presented as part of a real-world [trail/route/activity] rather than as a clear video game, I would rate it No.

Instruction-reference variant:
> The main offer is [real-world activity] with a companion [quiz/app] component. Quizzes are not explicitly mentioned in the instructions, so I think the rating depends on how the quiz is framed. Since it is framed as part of a real-world [trail/activity] rather than as a clear video game, I would rate it No.

Game-like wording variant:
> This uses some game-like [quiz/challenge/AR] wording, but the main offer appears to be [real-world activity]. Since the [quiz/app/AR] element supports a real-world experience rather than clearly advertising a video game, I would rate it No.

Short variant:
> The main offer appears to be [real-world activity] with a companion [quiz/app] element, not a clear video game. I would rate it No.

AR/trail-specific variant:
> The creative mentions [AR trails / quiz trails], but the offer appears to be a real-world trail experience with a digital companion. Since the main product is not clearly a video game, I would rate it No.

Research-aware variant:
> [Product/brand] is presented as a [real-world trail/activity], with the [quiz/app/AR] element serving as a companion. I did not find evidence that the digital component is separately promoted as a video game, so I would rate it No.

Phrases to use carefully:
- “standalone video game” — use carefully, because some valid video games are not standalone. “Clear video game” or “clearly framed as a video game” is usually safer.
- “framed” — useful for quiz borderline cases, but avoid repeating it too often in the same comment.
- “quiz app” — do not imply quiz apps can never be games. Pair it with the real-world trail/activity framing.
- “game-like” — useful for acknowledging ambiguity, but explain why the signal is not enough.
- “AR” — do not treat AR as automatically game-related; AR may support real-world tourism, education, navigation, or museum-style experiences.

Notes:
- This pattern is strongest when the comment names both sides: the game-like companion element and the real-world main offer.
- Research all visible product, advertiser, route, app, and branded-phrase candidates before using the No wording. If research identifies the app itself as the promoted game, use a research-resolved pattern instead.
- Use the instruction-reference variant when the borderline issue is specifically about whether quizzes count under the instructions.
- Use the shorter variant when the visible real-world framing is already obvious.
- Do not over-explain the whole framework in the comment. One to three sentences is usually enough.
- If the visible content clearly presents the app itself as the playable digital game, do not use this No pattern.

### 1.2 Research-resolved entity or meaning

Use when research into a visible name, advertiser, company, publisher, developer, brand, product, app, platform, service, site/domain, title, slogan, acronym, specialist term, or potentially meaningful ordinary-looking phrase materially resolves the subject.

Approved base template:
> [Entity/phrase] appears to be [verified identity or meaning], so I would rate it [rating].

Direct verified-identity variant:
> [Entity] is a [verified product/company type], which places the promotion under [covered Video Games area].

Sparse-creative variant:
> The creative itself is sparse, but [Entity] appears to be a [verified product type], so I would rate it [rating].

Ordinary-looking phrase variant:
> “[Visible phrase]” could look generic here, but it is also the name of [verified game/product/reference]. Together with [supporting task clue], that points to [classification].

Official-source variant:
> [Official source type] describes [Entity] as [verified identity/product type], so I would rate the promotion [rating].

Non-game resolution variant:
> [Entity] appears to be a [verified non-game product type]. The [game-like visible clue] does not change the main product, so I would rate it No.

Short product variants:
> [Product] appears to be a premium mobile gaming portal.

> [Product] appears to be an online games site / game-download platform.

Phrases to use carefully:
- “gaming platform” — useful, but broad. Prefer “mobile gaming portal,” “online games site,” “game-download platform,” “developer,” or “publisher” when the source supports a more specific label.
- “appears to be” — good when the identity match is reliable but the comment should preserve some distance from external research.
- “is” — use when an authoritative source and the task identity match make the fact clear.
- “based on its landing page” — avoid unless that page was actually inspected and is the basis. “Its official site describes…” or “research identifies…” is usually more precise.
- “content platform” — too broad by itself unless paired with the relevant game or non-game type.

Notes:
- This pattern is not limited to sparse creatives or positive ratings. Research can resolve the task toward Yes, No, or continued uncertainty.
- Name the identity or meaning that matters; do not merely say the product is “related.”
- Research every plausible candidate, including phrases that might have both an ordinary reading and a product/title/reference meaning.
- Use only findings tied to the exact visible entity. Do not import a same-named product or infer unseen post-click behavior.
- Keep the research wording short when the identity and category consequence are clear.

### 1.3 Game-publisher promotion without a specific title

Use when the company or brand itself is the central promoted subject, research verifies it as a video-game developer or publisher, and no individual game is named.

Approved base template:
> [Company] is a [mobile/video-game] developer and publisher, so the promotion is related to video-game publishing even though it does not name a specific game.

Short variant:
> [Company] is a video-game developer and publisher. Since the company itself is being promoted, I would rate it Yes even without a specific game title.

Official-scope variant:
> Research identifies [Company] as a video-game [developer/publisher]. The instructions include video-game publishing and marketing, so I would rate this company-level promotion Yes.

Brand-led variant:
> The creative is centered on [Company], which is a verified [mobile-game/video-game] publisher. A specific title is not necessary here because the promoted subject is the game publisher itself.

Phrases to use carefully:
- “game company” — understandable but vague; use “video-game developer,” “publisher,” or both when verified.
- “even without a specific game” — use only when the company/publisher is the main promoted subject.
- “publisher promotion” — do not use when the publisher name is merely a small advertiser label for a separate non-game product.

Notes:
- Verify the exact company and its developer/publisher role before using this pattern.
- The decisive distinction is central company-level promotion versus incidental advertiser branding.
- If a separate non-game product is clearly promoted, use wording for that main product instead; the advertiser’s other game business does not control the rating.

### 1.4 Quiz framing: generic trivia quiz without clear game framing

Use when the task shows a quiz or trivia prompt, research does not identify it as a covered game product, and the remaining framing does not clearly present it as a video game, game app, online game, downloadable game, or gaming platform.

Approved base template:
> Quizzes are not explicitly mentioned in the instructions, so I think the rating depends on how the quiz is framed. This creative shows a [type of quiz], but neither the visible framing nor the researched identity clearly presents it as a video game. I would rate it No.

Short variant:
> This shows a [type of quiz], but it is not clearly framed or identified as a video game or game app. Since quiz-style interaction alone is not enough, I would rate it No.

Useful Yes-with-uncertainty contrast variant:
> This looks more like a casual trivia game than a generic quiz, and [researched clue] supports that interpretation. The creative is not explicit about the exact game or platform, so I would rate it Yes with some uncertainty.

Phrases to use carefully:
- “quiz game” — only use when the task evidence supports a game interpretation, not just because there are answer buttons.
- “challenge” — a challenge can be game-like, but it is not enough by itself for Video Games.
- “test or assessment” — use carefully, because trivia quizzes are also a kind of test. If needed, distinguish “generic quiz/test framing” from “video-game framing.”
- “Quizzes are not explicitly mentioned in the instructions” — useful for explaining the borderline issue, but pair it with the task-specific reason for the rating.

Notes:
- The strongest No comments mention both parts: the instructions do not clearly cover quizzes by themselves, and the completed evidence/research check does not add clear game/app framing.
- Research every visible quiz title, advertiser, brand, URL/domain, and distinctive phrase before using this No pattern.
- Keep the comment grounded in visible details such as the question format, answer buttons, lack of game framing, and any verified identity finding.
- Do not over-explain the full framework in the rating comment. One or two sentences is usually enough.

### 1.5 Sparse researched game with supporting mechanic wording

Use when the creative itself is sparse, but research identifies a visible title or potentially title-like phrase as a digital game, and the visible wording also lightly supports the verified mechanic.

Approved base template:
> “[Product]” appears to be a [digital puzzle/mobile/online] game. The creative is sparse, but the “[visible wording]” wording makes it look like a mobile or online game.

Optional variants:
> “[Product]” appears to be a digital puzzle game. The creative itself is minimal, but “[visible wording]” is consistent with a simple casual game mechanic.

> The creative is sparse, but “[Product]” appears to be a [game type], and the “[visible wording]” line supports that interpretation.

> Based on the named product, “[Product]” appears to be a [digital puzzle/mobile/online] game. The visible “[visible wording]” wording also points in that direction.

Short variant:
> “[Product]” appears to be a digital puzzle game, and the sparse creative’s “[visible wording]” line fits that kind of mobile/online game.

Phrases to use carefully:
- “makes it look like” — natural and useful for uncertainty, but should be paired with the researched product type when research is the main basis.
- “digital puzzle game” — use only when research supports that specific product type.
- “mobile or online game” — useful when the exact platform is not fully visible, but avoid using it if research only supports one specific platform.
- “Sort/Match/Solve wording” — do not treat mechanic-like wording as enough by itself unless the named product is clearly game-related.

Notes:
- This is a specialized variant of the research-resolved pattern. The researched identity is primary; the matching visible mechanic wording is secondary.
- Research object-plus-action or otherwise ordinary-looking phrases instead of assuming they are merely generic copy.
- Keep the comment short. The useful structure is: researched product type first, sparse visible support second.
- Do not say the creative “clearly shows a game” when the actual screenshot is minimal. Use “appears to be” or “makes it look like” to preserve the uncertainty.

### 1.6 Unresolved app downloader after advertiser and product research

Use when an app-download or app-downloader creative does not identify the promoted app/service, a game-branded advertiser such as “123 Games” is the only remaining game signal, and research into the advertiser, product clues, URL/domain, and visible wording still does not resolve the offer.

Approved base template:
> The creative does not clearly indicate what is being advertised. “[Advertiser name]” could point to video games, but research did not reliably identify the promoted app or service, so there is still too little information to rate it clearly either way.

Optional variants:
> The creative only shows a generic app-download offer. Although “[advertiser name]” could suggest a connection to video games, I could not verify what app or service this particular ad promotes, so I would mark it Unrateable.

> “[Advertiser name]” may be game-related, but that alone does not identify the product in this creative. Since research did not establish a game app or game-download service, I do not think there is enough information to classify it either way.

Short variant:
> The product remains unidentified after research. “[Advertiser name]” is a possible game signal, but it is not enough by itself, so I would mark the creative Unrateable.

Phrases to use carefully:
- “could point to video games” — useful for an unresolved advertiser-name signal, but pair it with what research did and did not establish.
- “not enough information” — specify that the missing information is about what app/product is being advertised.
- “app downloader” — do not treat this as automatically game-related; explain whether the task evidence ties it to games.
- “very plausibly” — avoid stacking hedges like “very plausibly could,” which sounds less natural.

Notes:
- This is an after-research Unrateable pattern, not a first reaction to a sparse creative.
- Research the exact advertiser, possible product wording, URL/domain, logos, slogans, and ordinary-looking title candidates before using it.
- If research identifies a game app, game-download service, publisher-led promotion, or non-game product, use the resolved pattern for that subject instead.
- Do not imply that advertiser branding is irrelevant. It is a real research lead; it is simply not decisive when its connection to the particular promoted product remains unverified.

### 1.7 Sparse creative still unidentified after research

Use when the creative is too sparse to identify the advertised product, the only possible game-related signal is vague wording such as “play” or “playing,” and research of every possible identity or secondary-meaning clue still does not resolve the product.

Approved base template:
> The creative only makes a vague reference to “[play/play-related wording].” Research did not identify a product or relevant named meaning, so there is still too little information to tell what is being advertised. I would mark it Unrateable.

Research-sweep variant:
> I could not connect [visible wording/branding] to a specific product, app, or game through research. Since the creative itself only says “[play-related wording],” there is not enough information to assess what is being advertised.

Short variant:
> The product remains unidentified after research, and the vague reference to “[play/play-related wording]” is not enough by itself. I would mark it Unrateable.

Task-detail variant:
> The creative only shows [generic CTA / vague play wording], and research did not resolve any visible clue as a game, app, advertiser, or product. There is still too little information to tell what is being advertised, so I would mark it Unrateable.

Phrases to use carefully:
- “what is even being advertised” — useful when the problem is assessability, not just classification uncertainty. It emphasizes that there is too little visible content to identify the product at all.
- “what is actually being advertised” — use carefully here, because it can sound like the product has several possible interpretations rather than there being too little information to assess.
- “vague reference to playing” — useful, but pair it with the missing product-identification reason.
- “not sure how to rate” — appropriate when the uncertainty is explained, but a direct after-research statement is usually clearer.

Notes:
- Keep this pattern focused on lack of assessability. The core reason is not simply that “play” could mean different things; it is that neither the creative nor research identifies the advertised product.
- Research whether “play,” nearby copy, logos, or other apparently generic wording could instead be a title, brand, slogan, campaign, or specialist reference.
- Do not use this pattern before the research sweep is complete.
- Do not use this pattern when the product is identifiable and the issue is only whether it qualifies as Video Games. In that case, give the best Yes/No rating and explain the classification uncertainty.
- If any visible clue is resolved as game-related or non-game-related, use the research-resolved pattern instead.

### 1.8 Game-like mechanic, non-game product framing

Use when an app or activity has a game-like mechanic, but the visible framing and verified product identity point to a non-game product type such as drawing, coloring, artwork creation, photo editing, or another utility/creative activity.

Approved base template:
> This looks like an ad for a [non-game app/activity type]. It has a [game-like mechanic] mechanic, but the visible text presents it as [non-game framing] rather than clearly advertising it as a video game.

Poly-art version:
> This looks like an ad for a poly-art drawing app. Poly-art drawing apps have a puzzle-like mechanic, but the visible text presents it as creating artwork rather than clearly advertising it as a video game.

Short variant:
> This looks more like a [non-game app/activity type] than a clearly advertised game. The [mechanic] is somewhat game-like, but the visible text does not clearly present it as a video game.

Visible-evidence variant:
> The creative shows [visible mechanic], which feels somewhat puzzle-like. But the visible wording focuses on [creating/revealing/editing/etc.] rather than playing a video game, so I would rate it No.

Research-aware variant:
> [Product] appears to be a [drawing/coloring/creative/utility] app. Although [visible mechanic] is game-like, the product is presented as [non-game function] rather than as a video game, so I would rate it No.

Phrases to use carefully:
- “puzzle-like” — useful for acknowledging the ambiguity, but pair it with why the mechanic is not enough.
- “does not qualify as a game” — can sound too broad. Prefer “does not clearly present it as a video game” unless the evidence is very clear.
- “in my opinion” — usually weaker than needed. Ground the comment in visible text instead.
- “drawing apps are not games” — too absolute; some drawing/puzzle apps could qualify if clearly framed as games.

Notes:
- This pattern is strongest when the comment names both sides: the game-like mechanic and the non-game framing.
- Research the product/app name, advertiser, URL/domain, specialist label, and any potentially title-like ordinary wording before settling on the non-game description.
- If research identifies the exact app as a game, use the research-resolved pattern or combine its verified-identity clause with only the relevant mechanic wording.
- Keep the wording short. The comment does not need to explain the whole manual; it only needs to say why the visible creative is not clearly Video Games.
- Use “visible text” or “visible wording” for screenshot facts; use calibrated research wording for verified identity. Neither permits assumptions about hidden landing-page behavior.

### 1.9 Cumulative implicit game framing without explicit game label

Use when the creative does not explicitly say “game,” “video game,” or the exact game title, but several visible clues and their researched meanings converge strongly on a covered digital game type.

Approved base template:
> The creative does not explicitly mention “[exact game/game label],” but the references to [visible clue 1], [visible clue 2], and [visible clue 3] strongly indicate a [game type] digital game.

Spider/card-game version:
> The creative does not explicitly mention “Spider Solitaire,” but the references to “Spider,” cards, card faces, and a challenge strongly indicate a customizable Spider Solitaire-style digital card game.

Short variant:
> The creative does not say “game” directly, but the visible references to [clues] strongly point to a [game type] digital game.

More cautious variant:
> The creative is not explicit, but the combination of [visible clue 1], [visible clue 2], and [visible clue 3] makes it look like a [game type] digital game.

Research-resolved clue variant:
> “[Ambiguous term]” can refer to [verified game/title/genre/mechanic]. Combined with [visible clue 2] and [visible clue 3], that strongly points to a [game type] digital game.

Opposite-direction variant:
> The creative has some game-like wording, but the researched meanings and remaining clues do not identify a video game. Since the main product is [non-game framing], I would rate it No.

Phrases to use carefully:
- “strongly indicate” — use when there are several reinforcing clues, not just one weak hint.
- “does not explicitly mention” — useful for explaining why the comment is interpretive, but pair it with the visible clues that support the rating.
- “challenge” — helpful as a supporting clue, but not enough by itself.
- “[game]-style” — useful when the exact title is not shown, but avoid overclaiming if the combined task evidence only weakly suggests the game.
- “can refer to” — useful when research verifies a relevant secondary meaning but the task does not state it explicitly; pair it with the other converging clues.

Notes:
- This pattern is for converging implicit evidence. It should not be used when the creative is simply sparse or unclear.
- Research every clue that could be a name, title, genre, mechanic, franchise term, or secondary meaning—including ordinary words such as “Spider”—before judging the convergence.
- If research alone resolves the exact product, the research-resolved pattern may be primary and this pattern may contribute only the clue-synthesis clause.
- The comment should name the visible clues, not just say the creative “seems game-related.”
- Use a cautious variant when the evidence supports the rating but does not make the exact game title fully explicit.

## 2. Core Writing Goal

Write like a careful human rater briefly explaining:

1. what the user appears to want,
2. what the result or creative appears to offer,
3. why that supports the rating.

A good comment should feel:
- specific,
- grounded in visible evidence and any verified research tied to it,
- concise,
- natural in phrasing,
- appropriately confident or cautious.

---

## 3. Use Plain, Concrete Wording

Prefer ordinary language over abstract evaluation language.

### Prefer
- “The user is looking for…”
- “The page shows…”
- “The creative appears to offer…”
- “Research identifies [name] as…”
- “This matches the product type, but not the brand.”
- “That makes it related, but not exact.”

### Avoid
- “The result demonstrates strong alignment with the user’s expressed intent.”
- “The offering partially fulfills the core objective.”
- “This exhibits limited utility despite thematic overlap.”

### Rule
Use the simplest wording that accurately explains the judgment.

---

## 4. Be Specific About the Decisive Reason

Do not stop at generic words like “relevant,” “related,” or “not a match.” State what specifically matches or fails.

### Prefer
> The product itself matches, but the retailer does not. The user asked for Walmart and this is from Target.

### Avoid
> The result is relevant but not fully aligned with the query.

### Rule
Name the actual feature that drives the judgment:
- product type,
- brand,
- verified entity or phrase meaning,
- retailer,
- size,
- model,
- function,
- missing claim,
- unclear evidence,
- substitute quality.

---

## 5. Use a Natural Reasoning Flow

A reliable structure is:

> The user wants X. The result offers Y. Since Z is preserved or missing, I rated it accordingly.

### Example
> The user is looking for creatine gummies at Walmart. The result shows creatine gummies at Target. Since the product matches but the retailer changes, this is a reasonable alternative rather than an exact match.

### Rule
Use this structure when helpful, but do not force every comment into the exact same wording.

---

## 6. Vary Sentence Length and Rhythm

Comments sound more human when they do not all have the same sentence shape.

### Prefer
> The user wants a 15-inch laptop bag. The page shows 15-inch laptop bags and cases, so the practical need is covered. This looks like a strong match.

### Avoid
> The user is seeking a 15-inch laptop bag. The landing page presents laptop bags and cases compatible with 15-inch devices. Therefore, the result satisfies the stated intent.

### Rule
Mix short and medium-length sentences. Do not over-polish every sentence into the same formal rhythm.

---

## 7. Use Certainty and Hedging Deliberately

Be direct when the evidence is clear. Hedge when the judgment depends on interpretation.

### Use direct wording for visible facts
- “The page shows…”
- “The title says…”
- “The product is…”
- “This does not mention…”

### Use sourced wording for researched facts
- “Research identifies [name] as…”
- “The official site describes [product] as…”
- “[Term] is also the name of…”
- “[Company] is a video-game developer and publisher.”

### Use mild hedging for interpretation
- “appears to”
- “seems to”
- “likely”
- “could still be useful”
- “may satisfy some users”

### Prefer
> The visible text is a little ambiguous, but it seems to point to a farming-themed game.

### Avoid
> This may perhaps potentially be interpreted as possibly related to a game.

### Rule
Hedging should clarify uncertainty, not weaken the whole comment. Match certainty to both the source quality and the confidence that the researched entity is the same one shown in the task.

---

## 8. Keep Professional Imperfection

Comments should sound written, not generated. Slightly conversational phrasing is fine when it stays clear and professional.

### Natural
- “not really what the user asked for”
- “still the same type of product”
- “close, but not exact”
- “the page gets part of the query right”

### Over-polished
- “insufficiently responsive to the dominant user objective”
- “maintains category-level relevance while diverging materially”

### Rule
A small amount of everyday phrasing makes comments sound more human. Avoid slang, jokes, and overly casual tone.

---

## 9. Explain the Judgment Without Over-Explaining the Framework

Use the rating framework internally, but do not turn the comment into a long policy summary. The comment should still explain the practical, task-specific reason for the rating.

Referencing the instructions is allowed when it helps explain why a case is borderline or why a category distinction matters.

### Good instruction reference

> Quizzes are not explicitly mentioned in the instructions, so I think the rating depends on how the quiz is framed. This creative shows a trivia quiz, and research did not identify it as a game product, so I would rate it No.

This works because the instruction reference explains the ambiguity, and the comment still gives the concrete task-specific reason.

### Weak instruction reference

> The instructions do not mention quizzes, so I would rate it No.

This is weaker because it relies on the instructions alone and does not explain how the visible creative is framed.

### Rule

Instruction references are fine when they clarify ambiguity, but they should be paired with the visible and/or verified reason for the rating.

---

## 10. Distinguish “Related” From “Useful”

A result can be connected to the query without being a good answer to it.

### Prefer
> It is in the same general area, but it does not really solve what the user asked for.

### Avoid
> It is related, so it should be considered useful.

### Rule
Do not treat topical overlap as enough by itself. Say whether the result would plausibly help the user.

---

## 11. Avoid Common AI-Sounding Patterns

### Avoid excessive abstract nouns
- alignment
- fulfillment
- utility
- correspondence
- substitution
- differentiation
- optimization

Use them sparingly and only when they are the clearest words.

### Avoid inflated phrasing
- “clearly demonstrates”
- “effectively addresses”
- “substantially fulfills”
- “strongly indicates” when only one weak clue supports it
- “notwithstanding”
- “commercially adjacent”

### Avoid mechanical templates
- every comment beginning with “The user is looking for…”
- every comment ending with “Therefore…”
- every comment using three perfectly balanced sentences

### Rule
Repetition makes comments feel generated. Vary the wording while keeping the logic clear.

---

## 12. Prefer Practical User-Perspective Language

When useful, explain how a real user would likely react.

### Prefer
> Someone searching for this exact model probably would not treat a different brand as equally good.

### Prefer
> Some users may still consider this, but it is not as close as the original query suggests.

### Avoid
> A subset of users may regard the offered alternative as sufficiently responsive.

### Rule
Use realistic user reactions instead of abstract audience language.

---

## 13. Better vs. Worse Phrasing Examples

### Retailer mismatch
**Prefer**
> The product matches, but the retailer does not. The user asked for Walmart and this is from Target, so it is useful as an alternative rather than an exact match.

**Avoid**
> The result demonstrates meaningful intent overlap while diverging from the queried merchant specification.

---

### Partial product match
**Prefer**
> This gets part of the query right, but not the full thing. It shows amber cabochons, while the user asked for an amber cabochon silver ring.

**Avoid**
> The page exhibits partial correspondence with the query because it satisfies selected product dimensions while omitting others.

---

### Weak substitute
**Prefer**
> These are related in a broad sense, but they are not interchangeable. Someone looking for a lawn mower is not necessarily looking for a recurring lawn-care service.

**Avoid**
> Despite thematic relatedness, the offering fails to preserve the user’s dominant functional requirement.

---

### Strong match
**Prefer**
> This is exactly what the user asked for: 15-inch laptop bags, with several options shown on the page.

**Avoid**
> The result fully satisfies all explicitly stated user requirements.

---

### Uncertain classification
**Prefer**
> The visible text is a little ambiguous, but it seems to point to a farming-themed game. Since the creative only indirectly signals that it is a game, I rated Yes with some uncertainty.

**Avoid**
> The content may plausibly imply a gaming-related experience, though explicit confirmation is not present, resulting in a cautiously affirmative judgment.

---

## 14. Drafting Checklist

Before finalizing a comment, check:

- Did I state the actual reason for the rating?
- Did I identify and rank all materially applicable signed-off patterns before drafting?
- Did I use one sufficient pattern or selectively combine only the best-fitting template, sentence, clause, or phrase components?
- If I combined components, does the result read as one coherent comment rather than stitched templates?
- Did I complete the manual’s research sweep before using a weak-advertiser, insufficient-evidence, generic-quiz, non-game-framing, or implicit-evidence pattern?
- Did I distinguish what is visibly shown from what research verifies, without implying hidden post-click behavior?
- Did I mention the specific match or mismatch, not just say “relevant”?
- Is the wording plain enough to sound natural?
- Did I use certainty where the evidence is clear and hedging only where needed?
- Does the comment explain the practical user impact?
- Could this same comment fit many unrelated tasks? If yes, make it more specific.
- Did I avoid overusing framework vocabulary?
- Does it sound like a person wrote it, rather than a polished generic assistant?

## 15. Default Comment Pattern

Use this as a fallback when drafting from scratch:

> The user is looking for [query intent]. The result shows [visible offer]. Since [key overlap] is present but [important difference] remains, I would treat this as [brief rating logic].

Research-aware classification fallback:

> The creative shows [visible evidence]. Research identifies [visible entity/phrase] as [verified identity or meaning]. Since [decisive category reason], I would rate it [rating].

Use the research sentence only when the finding materially supports the rating. Omit it when visible evidence already makes the decisive reason clear.

### Example
> The user is looking for a specific Nike running shoe model. The result shows Adidas running shoes instead. Since it preserves the broad product need but changes the requested brand and model, it works as a substitute rather than an exact match.
