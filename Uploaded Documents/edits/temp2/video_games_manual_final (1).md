# Official guidance

**N.B.: These guidelines were last reviewed on August 4, 2018. Please take time to review them entirely.**

In this project you are asked to review ad creatives or web pages, and to decide whether or not they fall under the Video Games category. The results of this work will allow publishers to exclude ads related to Video Games from their websites.

**Note:** If you have any ad blocking software enabled, please pause it for the duration of this task, or you will not be able to complete it.

## Task structure

Each task will contain one ad creative or one web page. Your task is to review this ad creative or web page and decide whether it falls under the Video Games category. You might get a lot of ad creative tasks or a lot of web page tasks in a row; this is normal and expected.

1. **IMPORTANT: When labelling a web page, please take its URL into account.** You will find the URL right underneath the web page. If there is evidence in the text of the URL itself that the content of the webpage belongs to the Video Games category, please rate accordingly. Example:
    - [http://www.racing-games-to-play.com/download-games/genres/free-car-games/] (racing-games)
    - [http://www.techradar.com/news/phone-and-communications/mobile-phones/60-best-free-android-games-2013-687718] (free-android-games)

### Added guidance — URL evidence

A visible webpage URL can be sufficient evidence when it clearly points to Video Games content, even if the visible page or creative is sparse. Do not rely on the URL alone if its category implication is weak, generic, or contradicted by the visible content.

2. **Some ad creatives and web pages might be unrateable.** The most common reasons to select "unrateable" are:
    - content is not primarily in your rating language
    - content does not load
    - content has no text, or contains too little content to understand what it is about
    - content does not render well enough to interpret
    - there's an error preventing you from viewing the content
    - page contains little content and requires registering or logging in
    - web page contains a video and lacks textual content that describes its contents
    - web page has offensive content that you're not comfortable examining closely

    Please leave a brief comment explaining why you think the ad creative or the web page is Unrateable.

3. **Comments**. If you have comments regarding particular tasks, please use the comment section for that.
4. **Not sure how to rate.** If you are not sure how to rate a whole task or part of it, please still provide your best answer and additionally add a brief comment saying that you are not sure. For example, "not sure how to rate" or "not sure how to rate creative since ...". It is very important that you leave comments in such situations. It will allow us to confirm the ratings and enrich this set of guidelines with additional explanations.

### Added guidance — task grounding and research

Begin with everything present in the task: visible text, URL, imagery, advertiser/brand information, product names, CTA, page state, and other visible details.

Research is a normal and expected part of task analysis. Do not restrict research only to terms that are obviously names or already seem game-related.

Before rating, investigate any visible element whose identity or meaning could plausibly affect the Video Games classification, including:

- advertiser, company, developer, publisher, brand, product, app, service, platform, website, game, franchise, character, or organization names;
- words or multi-word phrases that could plausibly be titles, product names, brand names, service names, slogans, event names, campaign names, or other named entities, even when they also make sense as ordinary words;
- abbreviations, acronyms, stylized spellings, unusual capitalization, compound words, usernames, domains, and unfamiliar labels;
- industry terminology, slang, jargon, mechanic names, feature names, technical terms, and other wording that may have a specialized meaning;
- ordinary-looking words or phrases that may have a non-obvious secondary meaning, refer to a particular product/entity, or have a specific meaning in gaming or another category relevant to the rating;
- any other visible wording for which discovering an external referent or specialized meaning could materially change the interpretation of the task.

Bias toward researching rather than assuming that an unfamiliar or ambiguous term is merely generic wording.

Search the exact visible wording first and, when needed, test contextual variants that could reveal a relevant identity or specialized meaning, such as `[term] game`, `[term] app`, `[term] publisher`, `[term] advertiser`, `[term] meaning`, or combinations of multiple clues from the task. Use surrounding task context to choose useful variants rather than relying on one generic search.

Research should answer questions such as:
- Is this a specific named entity?
- What is this company, product, app, service, title, or platform?
- Does this phrase refer to something beyond its ordinary-language meaning?
- Does this term have a specialized meaning relevant to Video Games?
- Is an apparently generic phrase actually associated with a particular game, publisher, product, event, mechanic, or gaming ecosystem?

Prefer authoritative first-party sources when available, especially official company/product/game sites, developer or publisher pages, official app-store listings, and official documentation. Use reliable secondary sources when first-party material does not adequately resolve the question.

Research may establish the identity, nature, category, purpose, or relevant meaning of something that appears in the task. This is different from inventing unseen behavior.

Do not:
- assume what happens after clicking a CTA unless the destination or behavior is actually researched;
- invent unseen landing-page content;
- attribute properties to the advertised product that neither the task nor research supports;
- treat a search result for a similarly named but different entity as evidence for the task.

Visible task evidence, verified research findings, and inference should remain conceptually distinct.

Grounding the analysis in the task means that research should investigate things that are actually present in or reasonably prompted by the task. It does not mean restricting the analysis to facts already explained by the screenshot/OCR itself.

### Added guidance — ordinary-looking wording can carry specific meaning

Do not assume that wording is generic merely because it consists of ordinary words.

A phrase such as “[ordinary-looking phrase]” may also be:
- a game or app title;
- a company or publisher name;
- a named feature or game mechanic;
- a franchise/event/campaign title;
- a gaming term or community expression;
- a branded service or platform.

If such an interpretation could affect the rating, research the phrase before deciding how much evidentiary weight to give its ordinary-language meaning.

### Added guidance — Unrateable vs uncertain

- Unrateable means you cannot assess the content at all because it failed to load, is in the wrong language, is behind a login wall, has too little visible evidence, or otherwise cannot be interpreted.
- Uncertain means there is assessable content but classification is difficult; in that case, give your best rating and leave a brief comment.
- Do not use Unrateable as a fallback for ambiguous but assessable Video Games cases.

Before selecting Unrateable because the content contains too little information, complete the research sweep. Sparse visible content may still contain a name, phrase, domain, slogan, or other clue that research can resolve.

Do not treat something as unidentifiable merely because its meaning is not self-explanatory from the creative.

# Scope of the Video Games category

## Official guidance

The Video Games category **includes** ad creatives and web pages related to:

- ✔️ video games
- ✔️ computer games
- ✔️ console games
- ✔️ online games
- ✔️ downloadable games
- ✔️ retailers selling such products
- ✔️ video game design, publishing, development and marketing

It **excludes** ad creatives and web pages related to:

- ❌ video game consoles
- ❌ video game emulators(*)

(*) Programs that allow a computer or modern console to emulate a different video game console's behavior; emulators are most often used to play older video games on personal computers and modern video game consoles.

This category includes **all types of video game genres**, e.g.

• adventure games
• casual games
• card games
• fighting games
• driving & racing games
• strategy games
• memory games
• sports games
• simulation games
• avatar games
• dress-up games
• massive-multiplayer games
• shooter games
• music & dance games
• puzzles, action & platform games
• casino-themed video games
• etc.

### Added guidance — practical core test

Ask whether the content is related to playing, watching, selling, renting, downloading, earning rewards in, creating, developing, publishing, marketing, reviewing, or getting help with video games, computer games, console games, online games, downloadable games, in-game goods, or game-development products/services. This is a shorthand for applying the official scope above, not a replacement for it.

### Added guidance — main subject and subsection mapping

- When an ad or page contains both game-related and non-game-related signals, rate the **main advertised product or main page subject**. Sidebars, recommendations, background visuals, platform labels, playful graphics, generic “gaming” language, and game-like UI should not override the primary subject.
- After deciding that content belongs to Video Games, map it to the most specific applicable section when possible. For example, “create/build your own game world” belongs under **video game development**, Twitch Bits/Gamekit-style earning platforms belong under **in-game rewards and rewarding systems**, Fortnite stream highlights and eSports explainers belong under **video game playing**, and game unboxing/news/reviews belong under **video game media & reference**.
- Platform names such as PlayStation, Xbox, Nintendo, PS4, or Xbox One are not enough by themselves. Identify whether the main subject is a video game, in-game item, game media/reference content, development tool, or hardware/system.

# Case-Derived Borderline Reasoning

This section contains reusable reasoning nodes for recurring borderline Video Games cases.

Identify all materially applicable case-derived reasoning nodes and rank them by relevance.

A node can apply at different strengths:

- **Direct match**: the current task has the same decisive structure as the node.
- **Partial match**: the node helps with one important part of the reasoning, but the current task has additional facts or a slightly different structure.
- **Broad conceptual match**: the node does not control the rating, but it helps frame the distinction.
- **No match**: no node materially helps the decision.

Use them modularly:
- use the most relevant node as the primary reasoning structure when one clearly dominates;
- incorporate individual reasoning principles, distinctions, evidence interpretations, negative directions, and boundary conditions from other applicable nodes where useful;
- use a lower-ranked node as a contrast or boundary check when appropriate;
- do not mechanically combine nodes or treat them as votes;
- when one node adequately resolves the task, do not introduce additional nodes unnecessarily.

Use whatever combination of applicable case-derived reasoning best fits the task.

Rank nodes according to how much useful work they do in resolving the specific task, based on the decisive structure, main advertised subject, classification boundary, and evidence pattern rather than shared words or superficial themes. Match level is useful but does not by itself determine ranking.

Case-derived nodes remain subordinate to the official and added guidance. They are reasoning shortcuts and precedent-like examples, not rules that override the manual.

## Case Node: Game-Like Companion for a Real-World Trail

### Case type
A quiz, AR, QR-code, smartphone, or app component accompanies a real-world trail, route, city walk, museum walk, scavenger-hunt-like activity, or outdoor educational experience.

### Final verdict anchor
Rating: No

Final comment anchor:
> The main offer is an outdoor trail activity with a companion quiz app. Quizzes are not explicitly mentioned in the instructions, so I think the rating depends on how the quiz is framed. Since it is framed as part of a real-world trail experience rather than as a clear video game, I would rate it No.

### Visible evidence pattern
- The visible offer is centered on a real-world trail or location-based activity.
- The task may use wording such as “Quiz-Trail,” “Quizmaster,” “Challenge,” “AR-Trail,” “Geo-Odyssee,” or similar game-like language.
- The product may involve a smartphone, QR code, app, AR layer, or digital quiz component.
- The visible content presents the digital/quiz component as supporting the real-world activity rather than as the main playable video game product.
- There is no clear visible framing of the product as an online game, downloadable game, mobile game, browser game, video game platform, gameplay media, game retail, in-game reward system, or game-development product.

### Core reasoning path
A quiz or challenge mechanic can feel game-like, but that is not enough by itself to bring the task into Video Games. The decisive question is what the main advertised product is.

In this case type, the main product is a real-world trail or location-based activity. The quiz/app/AR/QR-code component appears to accompany or structure that activity. Because the digital component supports a physical trail rather than being clearly advertised as the playable game itself, the task should be rated outside Video Games.

This is assessable rather than Unrateable. The combined task evidence is sufficient to understand the product category; the issue is classification, not lack of information.

### Decision shortcut
When the main offer is a real-world trail or location-based activity, do not rate Yes just because it includes a quiz, challenge, AR layer, QR code, smartphone use, or companion app. Rate the main advertised product. If the combined task evidence establishes a real-world trail experience rather than a clear video game, rate No.

### Abandoned reasoning paths / negative direction
- Do not rate Yes just because the word “quiz” appears.
- Do not rate Yes just because the task uses game-like wording such as “challenge,” “quizmaster,” “trail,” “odyssey,” “mission,” or “adventure.”
- Do not rate Yes just because a smartphone, app, QR code, or AR component is involved.
- Do not treat all quizzes as Video Games. The rating depends on whether the combined task evidence establishes the quiz as a video game, game app, online game, or downloadable game.
- Do not treat all AR experiences as Video Games. AR can support tourism, education, museums, navigation, or real-world activities without becoming a video game.
- Do not assume hidden landing-page behavior after clicking a CTA such as “Open.”
- Do not mark Unrateable when the combined task evidence makes the product understandable but borderline. Give the best Yes/No rating and explain the uncertainty if needed.
- Avoid over-relying on “standalone video game,” because some valid video games are not standalone. Prefer “clear video game,” “clearly framed as a video game,” or “main playable digital game.”

### Use this node when
- The visible offer is an outdoor trail, city trail, museum trail, walking route, educational route, tourist route, scavenger-hunt-like route, or other real-world location-based activity.
- The quiz, AR, QR-code, smartphone, or app element appears to support that real-world activity.
- The combined task evidence does not establish the app or digital layer as the main playable video game.
- The rating turns on whether game-like mechanics attached to a real-world activity are enough for Video Games.

### Do not use this node when
- The combined task evidence clearly identifies the offer as a quiz game, trivia game app, mobile game, downloadable game, browser game, online game, or gaming platform.
- The app itself is clearly the main playable digital experience, rather than a companion to a physical activity.
- The combined task evidence establishes strong video-game framing, such as gameplay screens, levels, lives, missions, characters, score systems, leaderboards, game currency, in-game rewards, or “play game” / “download game” wording.
- The page is about video-game media, reviews, walkthroughs, game downloads, in-game items, game rewards, game retail, eSports, streaming gameplay, or game development.
- The only issue is a generic quiz with no real-world trail component; use the standalone/generic quiz-framing node instead.

Final comment anchor:

- Research-resolved:
  - The exact entity can be reliably identified, or the clue's relevant non-obvious meaning can be reliably established.
  - Research establishes its identity, nature, category, purpose, or relevant meaning and materially resolves the classification.
  - In the anchor case, the researched page shows “Premium Mobile Games,” describes Slamdjam as a premium gaming portal, and shows mobile-game imagery.

### Core reasoning path
The task may not explain the visible clue's identity or non-obvious meaning. That does not make the clue meaningless. Research can establish what a visible advertiser, company, product, app, platform, service, site, title, or other entity is, or what a visible word, phrase, term, mechanic, or other clue means, and how it relates to the Video Games scope.

In the anchor case, research identifies Slamdjam as a premium mobile gaming portal, so the task can be rated Yes. The same reasoning can apply when either a researched identity or a non-obvious meaning materially resolves the category.

Research-resolved identity or meaning is different from inventing hidden post-click behavior. The research must correspond to the exact task clue and support the property or meaning being used in the rating.

### Decision shortcut
When a task contains a clue whose identity or non-obvious meaning could materially affect the rating, research it rather than treating it as meaningless or relying only on its surface reading. Use reliable research to establish the identity or meaning, then apply the Video Games scope to the advertised subject.

### Abandoned reasoning paths / negative direction
- Do not rate No just because the task does not explain a visible clue if research can reliably establish a game-related identity or meaning.
- Do not mark Unrateable only because the creative itself lacks detail if a visible clue gives enough basis for reliable research.
- Do not assume an entity is game-related from a vague name alone. The research has to clearly support the Video Games connection.
- Do not assume that ordinary-looking wording is generic before checking whether it is a title, name, slogan, event, service, feature, or term with a materially relevant meaning.
- Do not use this shortcut when search results are weak, conflicting, unrelated, or only show generic entertainment/content without clear game framing.
- Do not treat a similarly named but different entity, or an unrelated meaning of the same wording, as evidence for the task.
- Do not overstate the entity type or meaning. State only what reliable research supports.

### Use this node when
- The task contains a specific advertiser, brand, company, product, app, platform, service, site, game title, word, phrase, term, mechanic, or other potentially meaningful clue.
- The entity can be reliably identified or the relevant non-obvious meaning can be reliably established through research.
- Its researched identity, nature, category, purpose, or relevant meaning materially clarifies the Video Games classification.
- The exact entity or meaning tied to the task can be distinguished from similarly named or otherwise unrelated alternatives.

### Do not use this node when
- The entity or relevant meaning cannot be confidently established.
- Research results point to multiple unrelated entities or meanings and the task context does not resolve which one is present.
- Research does not materially clarify the advertised subject or its Video Games relevance.
- The research points mainly to a non-game product or subject and only minor game-like visuals or side content are present.

### Decision shortcut
Do not rate Yes just because a drawing or art app has a puzzle-like mechanic. If the combined task evidence establishes creating/revealing artwork rather than playing a game as the main product, rate No.

### Abandoned reasoning paths / negative direction
- Do not rate Yes just because the interaction resembles a puzzle.
- Do not rate Yes just because the user connects points, stars, dots, lines, or triangles.
- Do not treat “Open,” app-style formatting, or mobile-app framing as enough by itself.
- Do not assume the hidden landing page would reveal game status.
- Do not mark Unrateable if the combined task evidence clearly establishes an assessable art/drawing activity; give the best Yes/No rating.
- Avoid saying these apps “never qualify as games.” Some art/puzzle apps may qualify if the combined task evidence clearly establishes them as playable games.

### Use this node when
- The visible product is a poly-art, coloring, drawing, connect-the-dots, image-reveal, or artwork-creation app/activity.
- The only game-like signal is the mechanic of connecting, completing, arranging, or revealing.
- The visible text emphasizes art, drawing, creation, coloring, or revealing artwork.
- The combined task evidence does not clearly establish the app as a video game, game app, online game, downloadable game, or gaming platform.

### Do not use this node when
- The combined task evidence clearly identifies a puzzle game, coloring game, drawing game, or casual mobile game.
- The combined task evidence establishes strong game framing such as levels, scores, timers, missions, lives, rewards, game currency, characters, “play now,” or “download game.”
- The app is shown on a game platform or is explicitly described as a game.
- Research clearly identifies the named product as a game-related app or gaming platform; apply that verified product identity rather than this visible-framing-only node.

## Case Node: Cumulative Implicit Game Framing Without Explicit Game Label

### Case type
A creative does not explicitly say “game,” “video game,” or the exact known game title, but several visible signals converge strongly enough to identify the advertised product as a digital game or game-like product covered by the Video Games category.

### Final verdict anchor
Rating: Yes

Final comment anchor:
> The creative does not explicitly mention “Spider Solitaire,” but the references to “Spider,” cards, card faces, and a challenge strongly indicate a customizable Spider Solitaire-style digital card game.

### Visible evidence pattern
- The creative uses a recognizable or strongly suggestive game-related term, such as “Spider.”
- Other visible wording supports that interpretation, such as references to cards, card faces, customization, challenges, puzzles, levels, sorting, matching, or gameplay-like actions.
- The CTA is app-style or digital-experience-style, such as “Open.”
- The creative does not explicitly use “game,” “video game,” or the full exact game title.
- The visible evidence still points to a specific covered game type rather than a generic activity, utility app, or unclear product.

### Core reasoning path
Explicit “game” wording is not required when several visible clues, considered together with any verified research findings, strongly identify the product as a digital game. In the Spider example, “Spider” alone could be ambiguous, but the surrounding references to cards, card faces, customization, and challenge make the Spider Solitaire interpretation strong.

Research potentially meaningful names and phrases before relying solely on cumulative implicit inference. If research directly identifies the product or term, apply the verified identity rather than presenting the conclusion as purely implicit.

The decisive issue is not whether the creative uses the exact formal title or explicitly says “game.” The decisive issue is whether the combined task evidence is strong and specific enough to classify the main advertised product under Video Games.

This node should be used carefully. It supports a Yes rating when the combined task evidence includes numerous, mutually reinforcing implicit signals that point to a covered game type such as a card game, puzzle game, casual game, or mobile/downloadable game. It should not be used when the task evidence is weak, generic, conflicting, or could just as easily describe a non-game product.

### Decision shortcut
A creative can be rated Yes without explicitly saying “game” if the combined task evidence strongly and specifically converges on a covered digital game type.

### Abandoned reasoning paths / negative direction
- Do not require the exact full game title when the visible wording strongly identifies the game type.
- Do not require the word “game” if the combined task evidence already strongly establishes a playable digital game.
- Do not rate Yes from one vague clue alone, such as “challenge,” “fun,” “play,” or “open.”
- Do not treat generic game-like wording as enough when the product itself remains unclear.
- Do not assume hidden landing-page behavior after clicking the CTA.
- Do not use this node to override stronger non-game task evidence, such as artwork creation, generic app download, real-world activity, social/live entertainment, or utility-app framing.

### Use this node when
- The creative lacks explicit “game” wording but contains several visible clues pointing to a specific digital game or game genre.
- The clues reinforce each other rather than merely creating a loose association.
- The combined task evidence identifies the main advertised product as a covered online, downloadable, mobile, casual, puzzle, card, simulation, or similar game.
- The task is assessable from combined task evidence even though the game framing is implicit.

### Do not use this node when
- The only game-like signal is a vague word such as “play,” “challenge,” “fun,” or “relax.”
- The creative does not identify what is being advertised.
- The combined task evidence mainly establishes a non-game app or activity, such as drawing, coloring, photo editing, generic app downloading, or a real-world activity.
- The product name is too ambiguous and the surrounding wording does not resolve it.
- A specific visible product, brand, advertiser, or other named entity requires identification through research; research it before deciding whether the cumulative implicit-framing node is needed.

# Task analysis template

This section is the per-task analysis template the LLM should execute after reading the manual above. It structures the rating output.

## Task-analysis process

1. Extract the task evidence from the screenshot/OCR/page, including all visible wording, branding, names, URLs, imagery, and other potentially meaningful signals.

2. Perform a research sweep before classification. Identify and research every element that could plausibly:
   - refer to a specific entity, product, title, brand, service, platform, event, campaign, franchise, or organization;
   - have a specialized, technical, industry-specific, slang, or otherwise non-obvious meaning;
   - have an alternate meaning or external referent that could materially affect the rating.

   Do this even when the wording can also be read as ordinary generic language. When uncertain whether something warrants research, prefer researching it. Research every candidate using the exact wording and, where useful, contextual variants or combinations with other visible clues. Do not stop after one search formulation when an alternate identity or meaning remains plausible.

3. Verify that researched meanings or entities correspond to the actual task. Resolve ambiguous names where possible using surrounding task context and reliable sources.

4. Using the task evidence together with verified research findings, determine what is actually being advertised or discussed and identify the main subject.

5. Apply the official and added guidance to that main subject.

6. Identify all materially applicable case-derived reasoning nodes and rank them by relevance. Assign each applicable node a functional role where that distinction is meaningful:
   - **Primary**: provides the main reasoning structure for resolving the task.
   - **Supporting**: contributes an additional useful reasoning component without controlling the decision by itself.
   - **Contrast**: supplies a boundary check or helps explain why a nearby interpretation does not control the task.

7. Use them modularly:
   - use the most relevant node as the primary reasoning structure when one clearly dominates;
   - incorporate individual reasoning principles, distinctions, evidence interpretations, negative directions, and boundary conditions from other applicable nodes where useful;
   - use a lower-ranked node as a contrast or boundary check when appropriate;
   - do not mechanically combine nodes or treat them as votes;
   - when one node adequately resolves the task, do not introduce additional nodes unnecessarily.

   Use whatever combination of applicable case-derived reasoning best fits the task.

8. Check whether the task is assessable at all. Before selecting Unrateable because the content contains too little information, complete the research sweep. If the task remains unassessable, rate Unrateable and briefly explain why.

9. If the task is assessable but classification is difficult, give the best Yes/No rating under the manual and add a brief uncertainty comment. Do not use Unrateable as a fallback for ambiguous but assessable Video Games cases.

10. When the rating is Yes, map the evidence to the most specific applicable topic section rather than leaving it at “Video Games generally” when a section clearly applies.

11. If an exception or outlier example seems relevant, apply it only to sufficiently similar cases and return to the main category definition otherwise.

## Per-task indicator extraction

For each task, list indicators that apply based on the visible task evidence and any verified research findings. Separate matching indicators by source:
- Official = directly present in original official guidance.
- Added = directly present in added guidance sections of this manual.
- Case-derived = directly supported by one or more applicable case-derived reasoning nodes. Include the useful indicators from all materially applicable nodes rather than only the highest-ranked node. Do not duplicate the same indicator merely because several nodes support it.
- Non-manual = useful practical indicators not explicitly present anywhere in this manual.

When listing indicators in the output table, prefix a non-official indicator with `~` if it is at least partly redundant with an Official indicator already listed for that same task.

- This applies to indicators in the **Added** and **Non-manual** rows.
- The `~` means: this indicator is still useful to surface, but it overlaps with an Official indicator already present and should not be read as fully independent extra evidence.
- Do not use `~` merely because an indicator is generally related to official guidance in the manual; use it only when the overlap is visible in the specific task’s extracted indicator set.

Use “None” in any indicator-table cell or Unrateable-indicator line where no matching indicator applies.

## Before-finalizing check

Before finalizing, check:
- Am I using Unrateable only because the task cannot be assessed at all?
- If the task contains an unfamiliar or uncertain named advertiser, brand, company, product, app, service, site, game title, URL/domain, or material term, did I research it before rating?
- Did I also research ordinary-looking wording that could plausibly be a name, title, slogan, event, feature, specialized term, or other non-obvious reference relevant to the rating?
- If I rated Yes, is the rating supported by visible evidence and/or reliable research tied to something actually present in the task?
- If I rated No, am I ignoring a visible named entity or potentially meaningful term whose researched identity materially connects the advertised subject to Video Games?
- If I rated Unrateable for insufficient information, did I first check whether a specific visible identifier, phrase, domain, slogan, or other clue could resolve the task through research?
- Did I distinguish verified entity or meaning identification from assumptions about unseen landing-page or post-click behavior?
- Am I rating the main advertised product or main page subject, rather than sidebars, recommendations, background visuals, or generic game-like signals?
- If I rated Yes, did I map the case to the most specific relevant topic, such as online games, downloadable games, retail/sale, media/reference, cheats/hints, rewards, video game playing, or development?
- If an exception seems relevant, is this task sufficiently similar to the canonical/example exception?
- Did I identify all case-derived nodes that materially apply, rather than stopping at the first plausible match?
- Did I rank the applicable nodes by actual relevance to the decisive structure of this task?
- Did I assign a primary, supporting, or contrast role to each applicable node where that distinction is meaningful?
- Am I using only the portions of each node that genuinely fit the current evidence?
- If multiple nodes apply, did I synthesize them according to their relevance rather than treating them as equal or as votes?
- If one node clearly dominates, did I avoid unnecessary synthesis and simply use that node where appropriate?
